THE LITTLE LANTERN

In a village at the edge of a wide green valley there lived a girl named
Mira, who kept the lamps along the road to the mill. Every evening she walked
from the last house to the river and back, and every evening she lit each lamp
with a thin wooden match she carried in a tin box.

"You are late again," said the miller, standing in his doorway with his arms
folded. "The road is dark, and travellers lose their way."

"The road is dark because the sun has gone," said Mira, "and I am late because
the wind keeps putting out my matches."

The miller laughed at that, but he gave her a small glass lantern and told her
to keep the flame inside it. From that night on, the walk was easier. The
lantern swung from her hand and threw a warm circle of light on the stones,
and the travellers who passed her nodded and said good evening.

One winter the river rose and carried away the footbridge. The village waited
for the water to fall, but the water did not fall. The miller's cart stood on
the far bank, and the flour could not cross, and the people began to speak of
the long cold months ahead.

Mira took her lantern and walked up the hill to the old stone house where the
carpenter lived. "Will you build a bridge?" she asked.

"The river is too wide," said the carpenter.

"Will you build half a bridge?" she asked.

The carpenter put down his cup and looked at her for a long moment. Then he
took his saw from the wall and followed her down the hill, and by the next
evening there were two half bridges, one from each bank, meeting in the middle
of the water on a wooden pier no wider than a cart.

The miller crossed first, and after him came the flour, and after the flour
came the winter, which was long but not unkind. And when the spring came and
the water fell, the village kept the pier where it was, and painted it green,
and hung a small glass lantern at its end, so that anyone walking home in the
dark could see where the road went.

THE FOX AND THE MAP

A fox found a map in a ditch. It was drawn on heavy paper, folded many times,
and it showed a country the fox had never seen: hills shaped like sleeping
animals, a river that ran in a circle, and a red cross in the corner beside
the words HERE IS THE TREASURE.

The fox carried the map to the owl, who was said to read everything.

"This is a map of the country east of the wood," said the owl, after a while.
"The river does not run in a circle. It runs south, and then west, and then
into the sea."

"Then the map is wrong," said the fox.

"The map is old," said the owl. "The river has moved. Rivers do that."

The fox thought about this for a long time. Then it folded the map, put it in
its coat, and walked east anyway, because the morning was fine and the walk
would do it good.

It walked for three days. On the first day it met a hedgehog who was digging
a new burrow and who said the river was two fields away. On the second day it
met a crow who said the river had been there a hundred years and had never
moved at all. On the third day it came to the river, which ran south, and then
west, and then into the sea, exactly as the owl had said.

The fox sat on the bank and unfolded the map. The hills were still shaped like
sleeping animals. The red cross was still in the corner. And the river, drawn
in a circle, looked nothing like the water in front of it.

"Somebody made this map a long time ago," said the fox, "and somebody believed
it, and that is why it was in the ditch."

It threw the map into the river, where it floated away like a leaf, and it
walked home along the bank, learning the country with its feet.

THE CLOCKMAKER'S APPRENTICE

There was once a clockmaker who had made so many clocks that his workshop
clicked like a field of insects in summer. He had one apprentice, a quiet boy
named Tam, whose job was to sweep the floor and wind the clocks that were
finished and waiting to be collected.

Tam wound them carefully, one after another, and each time he wound a clock he
wondered why the small hand moved so slowly while the long hand raced around
the face, and why the pendulum in the corner swung once for every second of
the world, no more and no less.

One day the clockmaker went to the city to buy brass, and did not come back
that night, or the next. The road was long and the weather was bad, and the
people of the town said the old man had surely stopped somewhere to wait it
out.

But the clocks did not stop. Tam wound them all, as he did every morning, and
each one kept its own time: the tall one by the window was a little fast, the
little one on the shelf was a little slow, and the old one over the door had
not been right in years.

On the fourth morning the clockmaker came home, wet and tired, carrying his
brass in a sack. He stood in the doorway and listened.

"Something has changed," he said. "The clocks are all agreeing."

Tam put down his broom. "I wound them every morning," he said. "And I set them
by the sun."

The clockmaker looked at the window, and at the clocks, and then at his
apprentice, and he began to laugh, because the sun had done in four days what
he had failed to do in forty years.

THE GARDEN THAT MOVED

At the end of a street of narrow houses there was a garden that nobody
remembered planting. It grew between two walls, and every year it was a little
different: one spring it was all poppies, the next it was tall grey grass, and
the year after that it was a single apple tree that had never been there
before.

The neighbours argued about the garden constantly. The baker said it belonged
to the house on the left, because the wall on the left was older. The
schoolteacher said it belonged to the house on the right, because the gate on
the right had a keyhole. The children said it belonged to nobody, which made
it the best garden in the street.

One summer the garden produced a great many tomatoes, more than the street
could eat, and the neighbours, who had been arguing for years, found themselves
carrying baskets from door to door.

"These are from the left wall," said the baker, "so they are mine to give."

"These are from the right gate," said the schoolteacher, "so they are mine."

The children took the rest, and ate them on the wall, and spat the seeds into
the garden, and the next year the garden was full of small bright flowers that
nobody could name.

THE LETTER

A woman wrote a letter to her brother, who had gone to sea, and gave it to the
postman, who put it in his bag and walked to the end of the road.

At the end of the road the postman met a fisherman, who said the sea was
rough, and the mail boat would not sail until morning. So the postman put the
letter in his coat and went home, and in the morning he walked back to the
harbour and gave it to the captain, who put it in a tin box and locked the box.

The boat sailed for two days. On the third day the wind turned, and the box
was opened, and the letter was given to a sailor who was going to the same
port as the brother, and the sailor put it inside his shirt and forgot about
it entirely.

Three weeks later, in a town on the other side of the water, the sailor was
sitting in a tavern when he felt the paper against his chest. He took it out
and read the address, and laughed, and asked the woman behind the counter if
she knew the name.

"He drinks here on Thursdays," she said.

So the letter waited behind the counter for four days, and on Thursday the
brother came in, wet from the rain, and the woman gave it to him, and he read
it standing up, and then he sat down, and then he read it again.

His sister had written four lines. She said the roof was fixed. She said the
dog had had puppies. She said the apple tree had flowered for the first time
in years, and she said she hoped he was eating properly.

He folded the letter and put it in his pocket, and ordered a cup of tea, and
looked out of the window at the harbour, which was grey and busy and very far
from home.
